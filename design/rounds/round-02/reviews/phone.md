# Review: Phone

I play with one thumb, in five-minute gaps, on a train. I weight legibility and
tradeoffs most, and I count systems. Every system a proposal adds is one more
thing I have to reload after a four-hour break between heat 4 and heat 5.

Two things I counted before scoring anything.

**Numbers a player has to hold.** The Ante has one: credits are money, score,
health and track power. The Toll has three plus a formula: points, credits, hull,
and intensity. Ignition has three: points, hull, credits. The Rising Line has
four: points, the line, Frame, hull — and Frame and hull are one letter apart in
meaning and will be confused on every single run.

**Rules that are not stat deltas.** The Ante, the Toll and the Rising Line all
run four tags with two thresholds each: eight rules, all live, all yours to
learn. Ignition has four, and you declare which one is yours in the lobby, so a
run teaches you one rule properly instead of eight badly. That is the largest
single legibility difference in the round and nobody else noticed they were
paying for it.

**Length.** Ante 8 minutes, Toll 7 to 8, Ignition 11, Rising Line 12. A
five-minute gap fits one Ante or one Toll. It does not fit a Rising Line, and
"two runs fit in twenty minutes" is a claim about a commute, not a gap.

## The Ante (economy-first)

**1 Build fantasy — 4.** Tags at two and three, and Sling 3 turning the run's
worst hazard into an assist is a real online moment. What lifts it above the
other tag proposals is that the market makes commitment contested: the third
Mirror Shielding costs 5, so going Mirror is a decision with a price on it rather
than a preference.

**2 Tradeoffs — 5.** The rising ante is the best forced choice in the round. The
cost of merely being alive goes up every heat, printed in the lobby, so the
question "can I afford this part" has a real answer every garage. Refit costing
your one purchase is the cleanest repair rule anyone wrote: repairing costs a
part, not 3 credits nobody notices, which is exactly what round one got wrong.

**3 Interaction — 4.** Two channels, and the market is the more original one:
"Ion Thruster 5 (base 2), three left" is counter-pick information as a number on
a card I am already looking at, not a spatial puzzle. It loses a point because a
ship below two of a tag has nothing to buy into the course, so in heats 1 and 2
most of the field has no move on the track at all — the same hole the round-one
critique opened.

**4 Stakes and recovery — 4.** Bankruptcy is earned, signposted from the lobby,
and gets its own screen naming what was sold. Recovery is structural: the tail
buys parts at base while the leader pays the premium the tail created. It is not
a 5 because there is no line drawn under anybody — "am I about to be out" is
arithmetic against a schedule, and I will get it wrong on a train.

**5 Legibility — 4.** One number is the strongest legibility premise in the
round, and "you go broke by building, not by losing" is a sentence I can hold for
four hours. Two things cost it the fifth. Fourteen board rows, each with a
current price and a base price and a copies-left, read every heat to make **one**
purchase — that is more reading per tap than any other screen here. And the board
runs in reverse ledger order while the course screen runs in ledger order: two
adjacent screens, two opposite orders, and I will buy in the wrong place at least
once a run.

**6 Fit — 4.** Pure sim, no contact, two to four taps a garage. Fine. The screens
are dense but the thumb work is light.

**7 Buildable — 3.** Seventeen PRs, first playable at nine, honest that
`hazards.ts` changes and that nine is not a handful. Credit for the honesty; the
number is still large.

**8 Measurable — 5.** Six assertions with numbers on them, and "the leader pays
1.5 more per part than the tail" is a real test of the market rather than a
restatement of the design.

**Best idea.** One currency for money, score, run health and track power. It
collapses four systems into one number, and the number is one I already look at.
Everything else in this round should be measured against how many numbers it
makes me carry.

**Fatal flaw.** None fatal. The nearest thing: the credit table that proves
"prices bite" assumes its own finishing positions, so the load-bearing economic
claim is asserted, not shown. The board screen is fixable and the two opposite
orders are one line of design away from being one order.

## The Toll (interaction-first)

**1 Build fantasy — 4.** Your build is your weapon: the pips that say what you
survive also say what you can put on the course. Mirror 3 buying uptime instead
of depth is the right rewrite — round one's Mirror was depth nobody needed — and
"the only ship that can be shielded through an asteroid field" is a set coming
online you can watch happen.

**2 Tradeoffs — 3.** Repairs at 10 credits against a tier-2 at 8 is a real price,
and the spender/saver/switcher table is the tightest of the four. But the toll
makes income unforecastable: I cannot decide save-or-spend at the draft when I
cannot say what next heat pays me. A tradeoff I cannot plan is a coin flip with
extra steps.

**3 Interaction — 5.** Every ship acts every heat, one tap, and it is the same
tap every time. That answers the round-one critique directly and cheaply. The
toll turning "hurt the people beating you" into income is the sharpest single
rule in the round, and reading the ladder's colour ribbon to predict the course
is counter-picking I can do at a glance.

**4 Stakes and recovery — 4.** Cut line drawn from heat 2, one ship per heat,
tiebreak printed beside it. That is the most legible elimination in the round. A
destroyed ship still collecting its toll is a genuinely good comeback rule and
costs nothing to explain.

**5 Legibility — 3.** Two screens and three taps is the tightest loop here, and
the Board — four hazard icons with pips and initials over a ladder — is the best
single screen anyone drew. Against that, the load-bearing rule has four
qualifiers: one credit per fifteen hull, off ships above you, as the ladder stood
when you staked, capped at five. I cannot predict it, I cannot check it, and it
keys off a snapshot nobody will internalise. And "the leader earns nothing" means
I win a heat and get paid less than sixth, which on a phone reads as being
punished for winning — the proposal knows and asks the owner about it.

Also, the cycle is not a cycle. Brick is beaten by Mirror and by Sling; Sling is
beaten by nothing in the table. Four arrows are claimed and three are drawn.

**6 Fit — 5.** Best fit here. Sockets are fixed level data, so the track work is
one number through `HazardContext`; the stake is one tap that is an input exactly
like a tap in the race; seven to eight minutes.

**7 Buildable — 4.** Fifteen PRs, first playable at nine, and the cheapest real
change set of the four because nobody chooses a slot — intensity is a multiplier
on hazards that already exist.

**8 Measurable — 5.** Six assertions, and measuring course variance across heats
is the right test for the risk it flags.

**Best idea.** Every ship stakes, every heat, with the same tap. One rule that
puts the whole field on the track without adding a screen.

**Fatal flaw.** The toll formula. It is the engine of the design and it is the
one thing a phone player cannot hold, forecast or verify. Fix it by paying a flat
rate — say 3 credits if your staked hazard was the biggest source of damage this
heat, 1 otherwise — and the design survives intact.

## The Rising Line (run-first)

**1 Build fantasy — 4.** Same tags, and the counter ring gives every build a
named enemy. Sling 3 removing the square from asteroid damage is the loudest
online moment in the round. It is diluted, though: half my attention between
heats is on Frame arithmetic, not on the build.

**2 Tradeoffs — 4.** Frame is the best long-run cost anybody proposed. One
falling number that says how much of your future you spent going fast, that never
comes back on its own, is precisely the F-Zero trade the brief asked for. The
economy under it is weaker: the table has the spender at zero credits after all
seven drafts, so for that bot the save decision does not exist at any point.

**3 Interaction — 5.** Eight cards into four slots in two waves, with the bottom
half seeing wave one, and Open Run as a defuse. That is the deepest track
interaction in the round and the wave-two information advantage is a real
comeback lever rather than charity.

**4 Stakes and recovery — 3.** The rising line is more interesting than a fixed
cut and strictly less legible: before a heat I cannot answer "am I safe" without
predicting seven other ships' finishes. The proposal admits 10/17/24/31 is a
guess against a mean that shifts as the field shrinks. On a phone I want to know
whether tonight is my last heat.

**5 Legibility — 2.** The heaviest by a distance. Frame and hull are two health
bars whose names mean the same thing in English, joined by a conversion rule —
hull repairs free to Frame, costing 1 Frame per 10 hull — that is a third thing
on top. The seeding screen needs me to tally eight cards across four slots,
resolve each slot to its majority kind, then subtract the Open Runs to get
intensity. That is a spreadsheet, and it is between every pair of heats. Twelve
minutes on top. The counter ring in the lobby is the round's best teaching
artifact and it is not enough to carry this.

**6 Fit — 3.** Pure sim and no contact, yes. One thumb, no: the seeding screen is
a screen to be read and reasoned over, not tapped, and twelve minutes is not a
gap.

**7 Buildable — 3.** Fifteen PRs, first playable at eleven — the latest here —
with Frame and two-wave seeding stacked on top of everything the others also
need.

**8 Measurable — 5.** The most thorough harness in the round: eight named bots,
eight assertions, and a distribution of cuts per heat, which is exactly the thing
its own biggest risk needs measured.

**Best idea.** Frame. Maximum hull that decays and never fully returns is the
cleanest answer to the brief's third gap that anyone wrote.

**Fatal flaw.** Two health bars and an eight-card tally between every heat, at
twelve minutes a run. Frame's idea survives without Frame's second bar: make hull
itself the thing that never repairs free, which is what the Ante already does.

## Ignition (build-first)

**1 Build fantasy — 5.** The best in the round, and it is not close. You declare
a Drive in the lobby, before a credit is spent, so the whole run has a direction
from the first screen. Cells are pure stats with a colour, so a pick is "does
this help" and not "what does this text do". Ignition is one visible flip with a
lit pip on the ladder, so everybody's build comes online in public.

**2 Tradeoffs — 4.** Ignition counting cells and not credits is a genuine fork:
four cheap cells light your Drive as fast as four dear ones, so buying cheap wins
the middle of the season and loses the end, when the only way up is to buy a
tier-3 and scrap a tier-1. Repairs at 8 credits against a tier-2 at 6 bites. The
three-bot table is the most convincing of the four because the saver loses for a
structural reason — it is cut before its build lands.

**3 Interaction — 3.** Only the bottom half of the ladder gets a move, and the
leader gets one tap. In the proposal's own worked run the player climbs above the
line in heat 6 and lays nothing: "climbing cost you your voice on the track." A
game that gets quieter as I do better is a pace failure, and it is round one's
critique with a new hat. The wipe is genuinely good — a move is lay **or** wipe,
so the last mover chooses between the course it wants and the course it survives
— and the course pick is real counter-play for one index. It is not enough to
cover a mid-field ship with nothing to do.

**4 Stakes and recovery — 4.** Fixed cut, one a heat, none before heat 3. The
head-start final is the best pacing idea in the round: the season resolves in one
race I can watch, and "I start 12 ticks back" is a thing I can see on a track
instead of a thing I compute from points.

**5 Legibility — 4.** Four rules in the whole game, one of which is mine, against
eight in every other proposal. One lit pip per ship says what that ship is. That
architecture is what I want. Two things stop it being a 5. Nine modifiers, which
the proposal admits it has not proved. And the collapsing corridor deals damage
that grows with time inside, which the current view cannot show — the proposal
names it exactly right: "unexplained damage, the one thing a phone player will
not forgive." Cut the corridor until there is a view for it.

**6 Fit — 4.** Pure sim, no contact, the head start is one number in `startRace`.
Three screens and eleven minutes is the longest thing I would still take on a
train.

**7 Buildable — 3.** Seventeen PRs and "four months at two a week" is the most
honest cost statement of the four. But the eight-PR first playable has no cards
and no course choice, which means it is the whole game minus the fourth gap — the
milestone looks earlier than it is.

**8 Measurable — 4.** Four assertions, thinner than the other three, and none of
them covers the lay-versus-wipe balance the proposal itself flags as a risk.

**Best idea.** Cells are pure stats; only the Drive changes a rule; you declare it
in the lobby. Four rules a run instead of eight, and the one that matters is
yours.

**Fatal flaw.** The player with the best season gets the fewest decisions. It is
fixable — give every ship a move and let position decide the order rather than
the right — but as written the reward for climbing is a duller game.

## Recommended merge

**Build from The Ante.** It is the only proposal where I carry one number, and
one number is worth more to a phone player than any two mechanics in this round.
The rising ante gives every garage a real question with a printed answer, and
Refit costing your purchase is the repair rule the other three needed.

Graft five things.

**One: Ignition's Drive.** Take the Ante's four tags and make them four Drives
declared in the lobby. Cells are pure stat deltas with a colour; only the Drive
changes a rule; ignition at four cells of your colour. This cuts the Ante's eight
threshold rules to four, and makes the one I need to know the one I chose on the
first screen. It costs nothing structurally — the Ante's tags map one-for-one —
and it is the single change that most improves the merged design's legibility.
The market underneath it gets better too: the board is now pricing my colour,
which is a thing I can see in one glance at four columns.

**Two: Ignition's head-start final.** One race, four ships, the ladder converted
to ticks up the course. Under the Ante the head start should come off the **bank**
rather than points — 1 tick per 2 credits, capped — which is more on-premise than
Ignition's own version and needs no second scoring system at all. The triple
final purse goes; the head start replaces it.

**Three: the Toll's every-ship-acts rule.** In the Ante only ships at two of a
tag can buy a marker, so early heats leave most of the field as scenery. Give
every solvent ship one move on the course screen every heat: buy your colour's
hazard, or **Hold for 2 credits**. Two options, same tap, every heat, and holding
is a real economic choice against a rising ante rather than a consolation.

**Four: the Rising Line's counter ring.** Four colours in a circle in the lobby,
each arrow pointing at the colour that beats it. One picture that teaches the
whole interaction game before heat 1 and answers the round-one critique's
complaint that no proposal ever named a Y that was not X.

**Five: the Toll's flat toll, not its formula.** Keep the idea that hurting the
field pays, drop the four-qualifier arithmetic: if the hazard you bought did the
most damage on the course this heat, take 3 credits, otherwise 1. It is
forecastable at the garage, checkable on the results screen, and it does the same
anti-snowball job because the leader's hazard is the one everyone built against.

Cut four things.

**Cut the fourteen-row board.** Deal eight rows face up with one of every colour
guaranteed, in Ignition's shape, and float prices only on those. Same market
signal, half the reading, and the price drift still says "three ships went red".

**Cut one of the two orders.** Board in reverse ledger order and course in ledger
order is two opposite rules on adjacent screens. Run both in reverse ledger
order: the tail buys its part first and its hazard last, which is one sentence
and still a rubber band.

**Cut Frame.** It is the best idea in run-first and it is a second health bar.
The Ante's hull — carries, never regenerates, and the only cure costs you a part
— already delivers the same F-Zero trade with one number.

**Cut the collapsing corridor.** Four hazards plus the Solar Flare is enough, and
the corridor is damage the current view cannot explain. It goes in "Later" until
there is a view that can draw it.

That merge holds at three numbers I ever look at — credits, hull, and a lit pip —
four rules of which one is mine, two screens between heats, and eight minutes a
run. That fits a gap.
